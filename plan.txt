Implementation Plan: Accessibility-Aware
Maintenance Allocation for India's Road­
Bridge­Culvert Network

 Oct 8, 2026 · @navdhd
This is a 24-week plan to test whether allocating a fixed  maintenance budget by loss of
access to health and school services protects more people per rupee than condition-
based ranking, with go/no-go at week 8.

1. Project definition

The project asks one question: does consequence-aware allocation beat the condition-
based ranking that agencies actually use, and does the answer survive uncertainty in
deterioration and repair effectiveness?

   Research question. Does allocating a fixed  budget by loss of access to essential
   services (health, schools) under monsoon-extreme hazards protect more people per
   rupee than condition-based and benefit-cost ranking, and how robust is that answer
   to uncertainty in deterioration and repair effectiveness?

Contributions to claim
 1. An accessibility-loss consequence model for Indian road­bridge­culvert networks,

    built from open PMGSY, OpenStreetMap and IMD data.
 2. A robust  allocation method (expected loss plus tail risk, rolling horizon) with regret

    analysis over uncertain assumptions.
 3. A reproducible benchmark: method validated on real labelled bridge histories (US NBI),

    deployed on Indian network data with stated assumptions.
Scope decisions

    Asset classes: roads, bridges and culverts only. No water, lighting or buildings.
    Study regions: one or two districts with different monsoon regimes, chosen after the
    week-1 feasibility check.
    Currency and costs: , from state PWD Schedule of Rates where obtainable.
    Human authority: the system recommends; an engineer decides. No autonomous
    execution.

5. Module specifications

Seven modules feed each other in a fixed order: network, hazard, consequence,
deterioration, fragility and cost, simulator, optimizer. Each has inputs, method, outputs
and tests.

5.1 Network build

Goal: a routable graph per study district with habitations, facilities and structures
attached.
 1. Load district boundary; clip PMGSY road centerlines, habitations and facility points.
 2. Pull OSM highways for the same area; prefer PMGSY geometry for rural roads and OSM

    for state and national highways.
 3. Snap and planarize (tolerance is a parameter, start near 25 m), then build an igraph

    graph with length, road class and free-flow speed by class (speeds are an assumption
    in the register).
 4. Attach each habitation and facility to its nearest node within a distance cap; log those
    beyond it.
 5. Attach structures from OSM bridge and culvert tags, and from IBMS records if you
    obtain them; split edges at structure locations.
 6. QA report: connected components, share of habitations connected, orphan facilities,
    tag coverage.
Output: network.parquet , graph.graphml , qa_report.md . Exit test: more than 95% of
habitations reach at least one health facility in the intact network, or the district is
rejected.

5.2 Hazard model

Goal: rainfall scenarios with known return periods for every road segment.
    Download IMD rainfall with imdlib ; compute annual maxima of 1-day and 5-day rainfall
    per 0.25° cell.
    Fit a GEV per cell, pooling neighbouring cells where records are short; check fit with a
    goodness-of-fit test and QQ plots.
    Report return levels for 2, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 100 years.

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            Page 6 of 19
Untitled

    Scenarios: (a) resample historical monsoon years as spatial fields, which keeps real
    spatial correlation; (b) stress events scaled to a chosen return level.
    Optional: test for a trend in the GEV location parameter and report it as a sensitivity,
    not a forecast.
Output: hazard_scenarios.zarr with N scenarios by cell. Tests: return levels increase
with period; fitted tails stay finite.

5.3 Accessibility-loss consequence

Goal: the person-days of access lost if an asset, or a set of assets, fails. This replaces
traffic-volume criticality.
For a failure set F, travel time to the nearest facility of type k changes for each habitation
h. With population P_h, facility weight w_k and a cap T_k on acceptable travel time:

  L(F)=\sum_{h\in H}\sum_{k\in K} P_h\,w_k\Big[\phi\big(\tau_k^{F}(h)\big)-
  \phi\big(\tau_k^{0}(h)\big)\Big],\qquad
  \phi(\tau)=\min\!\big(\tau/T_k,\,1\big)

Here  is travel time on the intact network and  with all assets in F removed; an
unreachable facility counts as the cap. Societal cost of an event is C(F) = D × L(F), with D
the outage duration in days.

    Non-additivity is the point. Two parallel routes each cost zero alone and a lot together,
    so L(F) is computed on the whole set, never summed per asset.
    Speed-up: run multi-source Dijkstra from facilities on the reversed graph, and only
    recompute for habitations whose shortest-path tree uses a failed edge.
    Population: use the PMGSY habitation population if present, otherwise a gridded
    population raster allocated to the nearest habitation.
    Weights w_k and caps T_k are policy choices; report results over a grid and show rank
    stability.
Tests (hand-built toy graphs): a single bridge on the only route gives loss equal to cut-off
population; parallel routes give zero individually and positive jointly; loss is non-negative
and monotone as more assets are added to F.

5.4 Deterioration model

Goal: a distribution over future condition for each structure, with calibrated uncertainty.
Validated on NBI, then transferred.

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            Page 7 of 19
Untitled

    Target: overall condition from the deck, superstructure, substructure and culvert
    ratings (check the coding guide for the exact rule), collapsed to four states: Good, Fair,
    Poor, Severe. Second target: time to Poor.
    Models: (M0) age-class Markov chain, the agency-style baseline; (M1) gradient
    boosting for next-year state; (M2) discrete-time survival hazard for time to Poor.
    Features from NBI itself: age, structure type and material, span and width, traffic,
    state or region. Add weather only if time allows.
    Split by inspection year, never random: train on early years, validate on the middle,
    test on the latest; plus a held-out-state test. Adjust year ranges to what you download.
    Metrics: log-loss and ranked probability score for state forecasts, concordance index
    for time to Poor, calibration of P(Poor within 5 years), and conformal coverage by group.
    Uncertainty: split-conformal intervals on time to Poor (class-conditional by structure
    type), plus bootstrap uncertainty on Markov transition rows.
Transfer to India. Map IBMS rating definitions onto the four states once you have them.
Apply an acceleration multiplier  to hazard rates, justified from published Indian studies
and swept as a sensitivity axis. If IBMS or PWD data arrive, recalibrate on a held-out slice
and report coverage before and after.

5.5 Fragility, outage and cost

    Fragility: probability that asset a fails in a monsoon event, given condition c and rainfall
    ratio R to its local reference level:

  \Pr(\text{fail}_a\mid
  c,R)=\sigma\!\big(\beta_0+\beta_1\,g(c)+\beta_2\log(R/R^{\mathrm{ref}}_a)\big
  )

Parameters per structure type (culvert, minor bridge, major bridge, road segment) are
assumptions with literature-informed ranges; you vary them rather than claim them.

    Outage duration D: a distribution by structure type, also in the assumptions register.
    Interventions: none, minor repair, major repair or replacement. Each has a  cost from
    the state Schedule of Rates, a labour requirement, and an effect on condition and on
    fragility.
    Emergency premium: a failed asset costs more to restore than a planned repair; the
    multiplier is a swept parameter.

5.6 Policy simulator

Goal: compare policies on the same futures.

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            Page 8 of 19
Untitled

One simulated year does the following: apply the policy's interventions within budget;
deteriorate each structure using the fitted model; draw a monsoon scenario; draw failures
with common random numbers; compute L(F), outage cost and emergency repair cost; log
everything.

    Run 5-year horizons over 500 to 1,000 scenarios; reuse the same random draws for
    every policy so comparisons are paired.
    Validation, component by component: deterioration against held-out NBI years; GEV
    fit against observed rainfall; accessibility loss against toy cases. No component is
    validated by the simulator's own output.
    Output: per policy, distributions of person-days of access lost and  spent, plus a tail
    metric (CVaR).

5.7 Optimizer

Goal: choose interventions that minimize expected societal loss plus tail risk under a
budget and crew capacity.
With common random numbers, whether asset a fails in scenario  under option j is a
fixed binary f, so the additive version is a linear program with binary decisions x:

  \min_{x}\ \frac{1}
  {|\Omega|}\sum_{\omega}\ell_\omega(x)+\lambda\Big(\eta+\frac{1}{(1-
  \alpha)|\Omega|}\sum_{\omega}z_\omega\Big)

  \text{s.t.}\quad z_\omega\ge \ell_\omega(x)-\eta,\ \ z_\omega\ge 0,\quad
  \sum_{a,j}c_{aj}\,x_{aj}\le B,\quad \sum_{a,j}u_{aj}\,x_{aj}\le W,\quad
  \sum_j x_{aj}\le 1

Here  is the scenario loss, c the  cost, u the labour need, B the budget and W the crew
capacity.

    Additive MILP (baseline): _(x) = sum of C_a times the failure indicator. Solves
    exactly with OR-Tools or HiGHS.
    Network-aware search: because real loss is non-additive, start from the MILP solution
    and improve with swap-based local search on the sample-average objective, evaluating
    L(F) per scenario on sets. Tune on 200 scenarios; report on 1,000 fresh ones.
    Rolling horizon: re-solve every year with updated conditions; compare with one-shot
    5-year plans.
    Quality check: on small instances, report the gap between local search and the exact
    solution of a linearized model. No optimality guarantee is claimed for the full problem.
    Constraints to include: annual budget, crew-days, a minimum protected access for
    health facilities (as a stated constraint), and optional fairness across blocks.

Untitled

Property tests: more budget never raises optimal loss; zero budget reproduces the do-
nothing policy; results are reproducible under a fixed seed.

Ablation: remove one component at a time from the full system (network effects,
uncertainty, CVaR, rolling horizon, rainfall hazard, facility weighting) and report the
marginal change in the primary metric.

Statistics

    Same scenario draws for every policy; report paired differences with bootstrap
    confidence intervals, resampling scenarios and, for cross-region claims, resampling
    regions.
    At least 5 seeds for stochastic searches; report medians and ranges.
    Pre-register the cost and weight settings before running E3 to E8; report any later
    change.
    Tune on one scenario set, report on a fresh one.

Robustness and regret. For each policy, report the fraction of sampled assumption sets in
which it wins, and its regret: the loss gap to the best policy under that assumption set.
The headline claim is a policy that is near-best across the whole range, not one that wins
under a single setting.

8. Product layer: orchestrator, API and dashboard

The workflow is a deterministic pipeline of tested functions; a language model only turns
finished numbers into readable text, which keeps every figure traceable.

Orchestrator

    A small DAG runner in workflow/dag.py : each node is a pure function with typed
    inputs and outputs, logged to MLflow.
    A request such as "what should we repair this year with  X crore?" maps to a fixed
    plan: load district, load scenarios, forecast condition, compute consequence, optimize,
    report.
    LLM role: narration only. It receives a JSON of results and writes the summary. A check
    rejects any number in the text that is not in the JSON.
    Human authority: each recommendation lists its assumptions, its uncertainty interval,
    and a one-click "inspect first" flag for structures where the prediction is uncertain.

Evaluating the workflow (small, honest)

    30 to 50 test requests with expected plans: check correct tool sequence and failure
    handling.
    Numeric faithfulness: share of figures in generated text that match the result JSON.
    Drop any claim comparing "multi-agent vs single-agent" unless you can run it fairly.

10. Week-1 checklist

compute one accessibility-loss number, while the data requests go out.

    README with the research question.
    Send a data request for bridge and culvert condition records to a state PWD and to
    MoRTH (RTI as a backup); keep a log of dates and replies.
    Download the PMGSY datasets for two candidate districts; note state-level quality.
    Download an OSM extract and count bridge and culvert tags in each candidate
    district.
    Install imdlib ; download one rainfall year and extract a district time series.
    Build the road graph for one district and attach habitations and health facilities.
    Compute accessibility loss for removing one edge; check it against a hand calculation.

11. Paper outline and demo script

The paper's argument is simple: condition-based ranking misallocates scarce budget
because it ignores who loses access, and a network-aware, uncertainty-aware method
does better across plausible assumptions.

Paper outline

1. Abstract and introduction: the maintenance-allocation problem in India, and why
    consequence is missing from condition-based ranking.

2. Related work: bridge management systems and deterioration models; accessibility
    and network criticality; conformal prediction; robust and CVaR optimization;
    infrastructure resilience under hazards.

3. Problem formulation: network, hazard, accessibility loss, interventions, budget.

                                                          Page 17 of 19
Untitled
